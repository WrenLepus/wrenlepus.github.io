Recently, I've realized my music has not been the truest it can be, and that for the six years I've been composing music, I could only count about one or two piece of music I find satisfactory to my standards and could be marginally considered "good". These two factors has, in a word, diluted my passion for music compositions by some degree and, simultaneously, thrown me into a broader reflection of what my music should mean to me. And, because one lifetime can never fit the myriad of music that exists and could exist, what makes a piece of music worth writing/listening to in the first place?

I've always had the way of approaching music as a subjective medium upon an objectivity, almost like a response. Creating music should be the product of us seeing the world through our own lens, the world which is true on its own, but equally true through these particular lenses. That's why music cannot be wholly subjective or objective and comes from the meeting of the two. If we start from ourselves as the root, the music might fall into the tendency to be a dogmatic explanation rather than to exult, to lament, to contemplate.

I've recently received a comment asking about my opinion on all the advanced/Avant-garde techniques for music (to name a few: multi-tonality, atonality, Serialism, extended techniques, etc.). My answer (for now) follows naturally from this view; if they are used, they should be written into the music with sincerity, demanded by what the music want to say at the moment. I think if one includes, let's say, an extended technique on the piano where the performer stands up from the their stool, and plucks the string just for the sake of it, the that has just became an end in itself; The music serves the technique, not the composer nor the listener. I think music should be true of the world, true of the moment, and true to oneself.

In no way am I saying music should return to its simpler, more traditional writing with proper voice leading and counterpoint, (Ahhhhh the good old days) or saying what MusicTM ought to be so and. The idea I am really sketching at is that of necessity and novelty. They are not directly oppositions, in fact, they can exist at once. Chasing theoretical knowledge might feel like progress, complexity might feel like progress, but maybe those pieces were up to my standards simply because I wasn't chasing some techniques or some tacky chord progressions mindlessly. Even now, I could still listen and hear the intention and reason of the choices I made on the score. Could that be the reason I find them "good"? I am not sure.

More reflections might be needed.
